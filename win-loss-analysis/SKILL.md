# Win/Loss Analysis Skill

## Overview
This skill helps product marketers design and run win/loss programs that actually change sales behavior and inform product strategy. Win/loss interviews provide direct access to buyer reasoning at the moment of peak clarity — but most programs fail because interviews aren't conducted well, findings aren't synthesized rigorously, or results aren't communicated in a way that drives action.

For understanding buying motivation and JTBD forces before a purchase, use switch-interview instead; win/loss is post-decision analysis.

## When to Use This Skill
- Win rates are declining and you don't know why
- Sales team is making the same mistakes repeatedly
- Product roadmap debates need real buyer data
- A specific competitor keeps appearing in losses
- You're entering a new segment and need to understand buyer criteria
- Building or formalizing a win/loss program

## Why It Works — and Why Most Programs Fail

**Why it works:** Buyers who've just made a decision are in a unique state. They've synthesized their research, done the comparison, and made a judgment. The reasons they articulate right after deciding are the closest thing to ground truth about what drove the decision.

**Why programs fail:**
1. **Sales reps conduct the interviews** — They have relationships to protect, deals to recover, and biases toward self-serving answers. Buyers lie politely to reps.
2. **Interviews happen too late** — Memory degrades fast. Interviews more than about 90 days after the decision produce rationalizations, not reasons.
3. **Only losses are studied** — Wins reveal what's actually working. Without win data, you can't distinguish signal from luck.
4. **Findings aren't actioned** — A 40-slide deck shared to a Confluence page nobody reads isn't analysis, it's data accumulation.
5. **Programs are reactive, not systematic** — Analyzing every deal when a competitor shows up instead of systematically sampling the deal population.

## Program Design

### Who Conducts the Interviews?
Candor scales with distance from the deal. In order of preference:
1. **Third-party firm** — most candid; buyers say things to a neutral party they won't say to the vendor
2. **PMM** — not tied to the deal, reasonably neutral
3. **Customer research function** — UX researchers are trained interviewers
4. **Never** the sales rep who worked the deal, or anyone who might try to recover the account

### Which Deals?
- **Random sample, not volunteer sample** — don't only interview happy winners and cooperative prospects
- **All deal sizes** — don't only study big deals
- **Balanced wins and losses** — roughly even is a good default; most programs over-sample losses
- **Recent decisions only** — within about 90 days of close
- **Enough volume to see patterns** — a few interviews a month is a workable rhythm; one a month is too thin to generalize. Starting out, a batch of six to ten per quarter establishes a baseline; a focused competitor or segment study wants a bit more.

### Recruiting
- **Losses:** sales ops pulls closed-lost deals by date; PMM or a neutral party reaches out directly (not the rep). Frame: research to understand how buyers make decisions in the market — not trying to re-open the deal.
- **Wins:** pull recently closed-won deals; a CSM or AE intro can warm the outreach. Frame: understanding what they were looking for and how they evaluated options.
- A modest gift card (say, $50-100) signals their time is valued and improves response rates.

## The Interview Guide

The guide below is a starting point for the human interviewer to adapt — reorder, cut, or deepen sections based on what the program is trying to learn. The section goals matter more than the exact wording.

**Opening (set the frame):** Explain you're doing research on how buyers in this category evaluate and decide — not re-opening the deal, not selling. No wrong answers; comments stay in aggregate research, no attributed quotes without permission; about 30-45 minutes.

### Section 1: Context Setting
Goal: what the buyer was trying to accomplish and why they started looking.
- "Start at the beginning — what was going on in your organization that led you to start evaluating solutions in this space?"
- "What problem were you trying to solve? What did success look like?"
- "How long had this been a problem before you decided to do something about it?"
- "Who else was involved in the evaluation — who were the key stakeholders?"
- "What was the urgency? Was there an event or deadline that created pressure?"

You're learning: the real business problem (which may differ from how sales characterized it), the buying committee, the urgency driver, and whether the deal fits your ICP or reveals a segment mismatch.

### Section 2: Evaluation Criteria
Goal: what factors drove the decision — both stated and actual.
- "When you started evaluating, what were the most important requirements?"
- "Were there absolute must-haves vs. nice-to-haves? What would have immediately disqualified a vendor?"
- "Did your criteria change during the evaluation? What shifted?"
- "What surprised you about what ended up mattering most?"
- "Looking back, what were the top 2-3 factors that drove your decision?"

You're learning: the actual decision criteria (often different from criteria stated at the start), which criteria differentiate winners from losers, what's table stakes vs. advantage, and where your marketing may emphasize the wrong things.

### Section 3: The Competitive Landscape
Goal: who they evaluated and how you compared.
- "Who else did you evaluate? Who was on your shortlist?"
- "How did you first hear about [your company]? And about [competitors]?"
- "How did [your company] compare to the other options?"
- "Were there capabilities or approaches that stood out — positively or negatively — with any vendor?"
- "Was there any vendor you dismissed early? What disqualified them?"

For wins: "What put [your company] over the top?" "Was there a moment in the evaluation where [your company] pulled ahead?"

For losses: "What made [chosen vendor] the right fit?" "Was there something [your company] could have done differently?" "What was the main reason [your company] wasn't selected?"

You're learning: the real competitive set, where you win and lose the comparison, what competitors do well, and whether you lose on product, price, process, or perception.

### Section 4: The Sales Experience
Goal: how the sales process influenced the decision.
- "How was your experience with [company]'s sales team?"
- "Were there moments where you felt very confident about [company]? Or where you lost confidence?"
- "Did you feel like the team understood your situation?"
- "Was the evaluation process itself easy or difficult, compared to other vendors?"
- "Did anything about demos, trial, references, or pricing discussions stand out?"

You're learning: whether you're losing deals the product could win, what builds vs. erodes trust in the process, demo effectiveness, and contracting friction.

### Section 5: Price and Value
Goal: whether price was a factor and what drove the value perception.
- "How did pricing factor into your decision?"
- "Did the pricing feel fair given what was offered?"
- "Were there surprises in the pricing discussions?"
- "How did [company]'s pricing compare to the alternatives?"

You're learning: whether you lose on price vs. on value perception, and whether your pricing model creates friction.

### Section 6: What They'd Tell a Peer
Goal: the most honest synthesis — advice to a colleague facing the same decision.
- "If a colleague said they were evaluating solutions in this space, what advice would you give them?"
- "What should they look for? What should they watch out for?"
- "If they asked about [your company], what would you say?"
- "Anything you'd do differently if you went through this evaluation again?"

You're learning: perception unfiltered by politeness, whether your believed strengths are how you're actually perceived, and the word-of-mouth narrative about your company.

**Closing:** Ask if there's anything they wanted to share that you didn't ask about, and whether you can follow up with questions in the coming weeks.

## Synthesizing Findings

**After each interview** (within a day or so, while it's fresh), write a one-page summary: what drove the decision (stated reason plus your interpretation), competitive mentions, criteria that mattered, sales process observations, price/value perception, most memorable quote.

**After a batch**, look for patterns:
- **Win patterns** — what's consistently present in wins but absent in losses: criteria where you lead, segments where you win disproportionately, sales moments that build confidence
- **Loss patterns** — criteria where competitors win, segments with below-average win rates, sales moments that create doubt
- **Surprise findings** — what doesn't match assumptions: a competitor you didn't know you were losing to, a criterion that matters more than you thought, a gap between what you believe about your product and how buyers see it

**Scoring and tracking.** Tag each interview: primary win/loss reason (product, price, process, perception, relationship), competitive intensity, criteria mentioned, competitors mentioned. Over time this yields win rate by segment and competitor, trend lines, and attribution — is this a product problem, a pricing problem, or a sales problem?

## The Findings Report

A win/loss report should be short enough to be read and specific enough to be acted upon. The enemy of action is a 40-slide deck.

**Executive version** (a couple of pages or a short deck): what we learned, top reasons we win, top reasons we lose, competitive dynamics, biggest surprise, and a handful of recommendations with owner and timeline.

**Detailed version** for PMM/Product/Sales: same structure with supporting evidence — paraphrased quotes, frequency data, per-competitor detail, sales process findings, product gaps, segment-specific patterns.

```
# Win/Loss Analysis Report
Quarter: [Q] | Deals analyzed: [N] | Wins: [N] | Losses: [N]

## Three Things We Learned This Quarter
1-3. [Specific findings with supporting evidence]

## Why We Win
### Win Factor: [Name]
What we hear: "[Paraphrased customer language]"
Frequency: [X] of [Y] win interviews
What this means: [Implication for messaging/sales/product]

## Why We Lose
### Loss Factor: [Name]
What we hear: "[Paraphrased customer language]"
Frequency: [X] of [Y] loss interviews
Root cause: [Product gap? Sales gap? Perception gap? Pricing?]
Recommendation: [Specific action]

## Competitive Intelligence
### [Competitor]
Win rate against them: [%]
What they win on / what we win on: [Top reasons]
Pattern/trend: [Change vs. prior quarter]

## Sales Process Observations
What's building confidence / creating doubt: [Patterns]
Recommendation: [Specific training or process change]

## Recommendations
| Recommendation | Owner | Timeline | Expected Impact |
|----------------|-------|----------|----------------|

## Next Quarter Focus
[What we're investigating next and why]
```

## Communicating to Stakeholders

Different stakeholders need different things:

- **Sales:** actionable intelligence they can use now — "when [Competitor] comes up, here's what buyers say they do better and how to respond." Deliver via Slack, sales meetings, or enablement sessions — not Confluence articles.
- **Product:** buyer evidence for roadmap prioritization — "buyers consistently mention [gap] when explaining why they chose competitors," or equally, "we're winning despite [limitation], so it isn't blocking deals." Frame as buyer evidence, not PMM opinion.
- **Leadership:** win rate trends and what's driving them, evolving competitive threats, strategic recommendations with expected impact.

The rule: if findings don't lead to changed behavior in sales, product, or marketing, the program isn't working. Go figure out why the findings aren't landing.

## Principles and Pitfalls

- **Interview within ~90 days of the decision** — memory is accurate; rationalizations haven't set in
- **Interview both wins and losses** — a loss-only diet produces a distorted view of what drives decisions
- **Keep the interviewer neutral** — the closer to the deal, the less candid the response
- **Share findings quickly** — a three-month analysis cycle is too slow to change behavior
- **Quantify frequency** — "7 of 12 buyers mentioned" beats "several buyers mentioned"
- **Make recommendations specific** — not "improve demos" but "revise the discovery question sequence for [segment]"
- **Protect neutrality** — don't share raw transcripts with sales; buyers were candid because they trusted the process
- **Don't let the program become a blame mechanism** — win/loss is about learning, not judging reps
- **Connect interviews to pipeline data** — interview findings plus deal data is where statistical confidence comes from

**The "we lost because of price" fallacy.** Price is the most cited loss reason, but it's often a proxy for "we didn't see enough value to justify the cost." When buyers cite price, probe: "If the price had been the same, would you have chosen [company]?" If no, the real issue is elsewhere. More generally, distinguish stated reasons from actual reasons — buyers sometimes cite price because it's easier than saying "your team didn't understand my problem."

**Analyzing exceptions, not patterns.** One vivid enterprise loss gets dissected while twenty mid-market losses accumulate unexamined. Sample systematically; volume reveals patterns.

**Findings that can't be acted on.** "Buyers want a better product" is a finding but not actionable. Push every finding to: what specifically, in what segment, driving what behavior, fixable how?

**Reporting backwards from the conclusion.** PMM already believes [Competitor] wins on [feature]; interviews confirm it; nothing else surfaces. Code interviews against a standard framework rather than filtering through pre-existing hypotheses.

## Working With Someone on Win/Loss

Start by scoping the question this round of research should answer, then design the sample and adapt the interview guide to it. During synthesis, help distinguish signal from noise and stated reasons from actual ones. The end product is a program that changes what the business does — improves win rates, informs the roadmap, sharpens competitive positioning — not a research archive.
