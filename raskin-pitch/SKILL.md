# Andy Raskin Strategic Narrative Framework

## Overview
Andy Raskin's 5-element framework for sales pitches and strategic narratives, based on his analysis of Zuora's sales deck and other breakthrough pitches. It replaces product-first pitching with strategic storytelling: name a change in the world, show the stakes, and position your solution as the way to win in the new reality.

## When to Use This Skill
- Creating a new sales deck or pitch narrative
- Revising underperforming sales presentations
- Developing product launch narratives
- Creating founder/executive pitch stories
- Repositioning products in competitive markets

## The Five Elements

1. **Name a Big, Relevant Change in the World**
2. **Show There'll Be Winners and Losers**
3. **Tease the Promised Land**
4. **Introduce Capabilities as "Magic Gifts"**
5. **Present Evidence You Can Make the Story Come True**

The sequence is the doctrine. Each element creates the conditions the next one needs: the change establishes stakes, winners and losers make inaction costly, the Promised Land creates desire, and only then do features register as gifts rather than a catalog. If your company appears before element 4, restart.

### Element 1: The Big Change
Start with the world, not your product. Name a shift that is external, already underway, and undeniable — the audience should nod along, not debate whether it's real. It must create genuine urgency (why act now?) and high stakes (what's at risk?).

Worth probing: what's different about the market today versus three years ago that customers would immediately recognize, and what's the consequence of ignoring it?

Red flags:
- "Our industry is changing rapidly" or "digital transformation" — too vague to carry a narrative
- A change only insiders can see, or one that finished years ago (no urgency)
- A change that is secretly "companies need better [your product category]"

### Element 2: Winners and Losers
Show that outcomes diverge based on how companies respond to the change. This is what overcomes loss aversion — inaction has to have visible consequences. Winners should be doing something genuinely different, not just "doing it better," and the losers' path should look uncomfortably like the one the audience is currently on.

Red flags:
- Winners and losers defined purely by whether they use your product type
- Differences described as "more agile" or "more innovative" with no specifics
- No real examples — the contrast feels invented to fit the pitch

### Element 3: The Promised Land
Paint the desired future for those who navigate the change well — described from the customer's perspective, specific enough to visualize, aspirational but believable. It should be hard enough to reach that they'll need help getting there, and it should naturally raise the question "but how do we get there?"

Red flags:
- Generic outcomes like "increased revenue" or "improved efficiency"
- The Promised Land is just "using our product"
- Outcomes disconnected from the change and stakes established earlier

### Element 4: Features as Magic Gifts
Only now does the product appear. Frame each capability as a gift that removes a specific obstacle on the road to the Promised Land. Features are tools for the journey, not the destination — each one should map to an obstacle the audience already feels.

Red flags:
- Feature lists with no connection to the Promised Land
- Capabilities any competitor could claim equally well
- Too many features — a few well-mapped gifts beat a catalog

### Element 5: Evidence
Prove you can make the story come true. Customer stories should mirror the narrative arc — a starting point like the prospect's, a journey through the change, arrival at the Promised Land — with specific, quantified outcomes rather than "great product, great team" testimonials.

Red flags:
- Metrics without context or a baseline
- A single example (could be an outlier), or customers too unlike the audience to be credible
- Outcomes that don't echo the Promised Land you described

## Common Pitfalls

**The change is really about your product.** "The world is changing because companies need better [category]" is not a market shift. The change must be real and undeniable even to people who've never heard of you.

**Winners/losers feels forced.** Differences invented to fit the narrative rather than observed in the market. Study who is actually succeeding and why; talk to customers.

**The Promised Land is vague.** "Customers will be more competitive" is not a destination. What changes day-to-day? Which metrics move?

**Features disconnected from the journey.** If a capability doesn't obviously remove an obstacle you've established, either cut it or establish the obstacle first.

**Weak or missing evidence.** Claims without proof read as marketing fluff. Show detailed transformations, not adjectives.

**Fake urgency.** "Limited time offer" is not the same as an urgent market change.

## Working With Someone on Their Narrative

Develop the elements in order — the later ones depend on the earlier ones. First answers are usually too vague ("companies need to modernize"); probe for specificity, but recognize a real answer when you hear one. Watch for inside-out thinking: the story is about the customer's transformation, not the vendor's product. When the narrative holds together, test it for coherence end to end — each element should flow into the next — then offer to translate it into slide structure or talk tracks.
