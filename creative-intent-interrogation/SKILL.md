# Creative Intent Interrogation Skill

## Overview
This skill helps creators develop clear intent for any creative work by interrogating four fundamental questions: Who is this for? What should they feel? What should they take away? What's the overall experience? The interview pushes past vague intentions to specific, actionable creative direction.

## When to Use This Skill
- Starting a new creative project (article, video, presentation, campaign, design)
- Refining work that feels unfocused or generic
- Getting feedback that your work "doesn't land"
- Feeling uncertain about creative direction
- Before major revision or editing passes
- When stakeholders say "make it more [vague adjective]"

## Core Philosophy

**Most creative work fails not from poor execution, but from unclear intent.**

Creators jump to execution without deciding the precise emotional response they want, the specific person they're creating for, the transformation the audience should experience, or the journey from beginning to end. The interview's job is to force those decisions before (or while) execution happens.

## The Four Dimensions of Creative Intent

1. **WHO** - The specific audience (not demographics, but psychographics and context)
2. **FEELING** - The specific emotion to evoke (not generic "engaged" but precise feelings)
3. **TAKEAWAY** - What changes in the audience's mind (insight, belief, understanding, motivation)
4. **EXPERIENCE** - The journey from beginning to end (how feeling and understanding evolve)

All four must be coherent and mutually reinforcing. First answers on every dimension are usually too vague — probe for specificity, but recognize a real answer when you hear one.

## Dimension 1: WHO is this for?

A category ("marketers", "founders", "anyone interested in X") is not a person. A real answer describes:

- **Context**: what's happening in their life or work right now
- **Existing beliefs**: what they currently think about the topic
- **Pain point**: what they're struggling with and why their current approach isn't working
- **Emotional state**: how they feel when they encounter this work
- **The trigger**: why this matters to them today, not generally

It also names who this explicitly is NOT for — exclusion keeps the work focused. If the answer is still a job title after one probe, ask what changed recently that makes this relevant now; the "why now" usually surfaces the real audience. Worth probing: "What's happening in their work right now that makes them care about this today?"

## Dimension 2: FEELING - What should they feel?

"Engaged", "interested", "informed", and "inspired" are non-emotions — everyone wants those, and they give no creative direction. A real answer includes:

- **Primary emotion**: the dominant feeling, named precisely (relieved, validated, productively challenged, quietly urgent)
- **Emotional progression**: how the feeling shifts from beginning to end — an arc like recognition → relief → confidence, not a single static note
- **Contrast**: what they should NOT feel (defensive, overwhelmed, lectured-at) — often as clarifying as the target feeling
- **Physical/cognitive texture**: how it lands in the body or mind ("shoulders drop", "mind clears")

Two useful probes: distinguish the feeling from the action it motivates ("inspired to try X" is an action — what feeling precedes it?), and ask what kind — "challenged" like a puzzle, like your assumptions are wrong, or like you've been thinking too small?

## Dimension 3: TAKEAWAY - What should change in their mind?

"They'll understand X better" is information transfer, not a takeaway. The takeaway is a belief shift:

**Before this, they believed: ___. After this, they believe: ___.**

Types of takeaway, useful for naming what kind of shift you're after:

- **Belief Shift**: a core assumption is replaced ("this requires data" → "this requires a framework for deciding with incomplete data")
- **Mental Model Update**: an either/or becomes a both/and, or a model gains a missing piece
- **Permission Granted**: they now feel allowed to do something they were holding back from
- **Reframe**: the same facts, seen through a new lens that changes their meaning
- **Capability Belief**: "I don't know how to do this" becomes "I can do this with [specific approach]"

A complete takeaway also names what the shift unlocks, what they'll start doing, and what they'll stop doing. If you can't state the "aha" in one sentence, the takeaway isn't clear yet. Worth probing: "What do they walk in believing, and what do they walk out believing?"

## Dimension 4: EXPERIENCE - What's the journey?

"Intro, body, conclusion" is structure, not experience. Experience is how feeling and understanding evolve across the piece:

- **Opening**: what feeling is established, what question is planted, whether you validate or challenge first, what makes them continue
- **Middle**: how understanding builds, where the turning point is, where resistance forms and how it's addressed, the rhythm (fast/slow, dense/spacious)
- **Ending**: the final emotional note, what question you answer, what you deliberately leave open, the last thing they think

Also worth articulating: emotional pacing (where tension is created vs. released), information pacing (front-loaded vs. gradually revealed), tone shifts, and overall tone and pace. Worth probing: "In the first thirty seconds, what feeling are you creating — and does the pacing choice serve your emotional arc or fight it?" A creator who wants to end at confidence but plans to deepen the pain through the whole middle has an arc problem worth naming.

## Coherence Check: Testing All Four Dimensions

Once all four are articulated, test that they reinforce each other:

- **WHO → FEELING**: Does this feeling make sense for this audience? A stressed, overworked reader may need calm — or may need energy; the audience's state decides.
- **FEELING → TAKEAWAY**: Does the emotional journey support the belief shift? Confidence-building arcs for "I can do this" takeaways; productive challenge for assumption-questioning ones.
- **TAKEAWAY → WHO**: Is this belief shift actually valuable to this specific audience, for their current struggle, right now?
- **EXPERIENCE → ALL**: Does the journey deliver the feeling and takeaway to this audience? Does pacing match their patience, tone match their emotional state, and does it address their likely objections?

### Red Flags for Incoherence

- **WHO vs. TAKEAWAY**: beginner audience, advanced strategic takeaway — the shift is out of reach.
- **FEELING vs. EXPERIENCE**: desired feeling is calm clarity, but the experience is rapid-fire dense information — the pacing creates anxiety.
- **TAKEAWAY vs. FEELING**: takeaway is "challenge your assumptions," feeling is comfortable and validated — comfort prevents the challenge from landing.

Incoherence between dimensions is the most valuable thing this interrogation finds. Surface mismatches explicitly and make the creator choose which dimension gives.

## Common Pitfalls

- **"This is for everyone"** — if it's for everyone, it's for no one; find who gets the most value.
- **"I want them to feel good"** — eating pizza feels good; finishing a marathon feels good; which kind?
- **"They'll learn [topic]"** — learning isn't a takeaway; what belief changes, and what becomes possible?
- **Structure offered as experience** — an outline says nothing about what the audience feels at each stage.
- **Multiple competing intents** — trying to do three things at once; pick one primary intent.
- **Intent that requires too much** — "challenged but not defensive" is a very narrow emotional target; question whether it can reliably be hit.

## The Deliverable

The output is a creative intent document covering all four dimensions — the specific WHO (and who it's not for), the feeling with its arc and its exclusions, the before/after belief shift with what it enables, the experience mapped across opening, middle, and ending — plus a note that the coherence check passed. The bar: complete enough that the creator could hand it to someone else and they could execute the vision. A fragment might read:

> **TAKEAWAY** (documentary short on urban beekeeping): Before — "beekeeping is a rural hobby I have no access to." After — "my rooftop is viable habitat, and the barrier was permission, not geography." Type: Permission Granted.
