# Category Design Skill

## Overview
This skill implements the category design framework from *Play Bigger* by Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney — the discipline of creating a new market category, defining it on your terms, and becoming its dominant company.

Category design isn't for everyone. It's for companies with something genuinely new: a different approach, a different problem framing, or a combination of capabilities that doesn't fit existing categories. Done well, it makes you the category king — the company that captures 70-80% of the economics in a market it defined.

## When to Use This Skill
- The product doesn't fit cleanly into any existing category — and existing-category positioning hides its value
- You're consistently losing deals because buyers compare you to the wrong alternatives
- Differentiation is architectural or philosophical, not just feature-level
- You want to define the problem before competitors do
- Dunford-style positioning keeps reaching the conclusion "we need a new category"
- You're a founder building a company narrative, not just a product pitch

## When NOT to Use This Skill

Category design is high-risk, high-reward. Don't attempt it if:
- You're competing in a well-defined, established category where "better" is a viable strategy
- You lack the resources to educate a market over 2-3 years
- The product genuinely fits an existing category and customers understand it naturally
- You want short-term pipeline results — category design is a 2-3 year investment

The commitment test matters: decide before you start. Category design requires a 2-3 year commitment at the exec level. If that's not available, pursue competitive positioning instead — `dunford-positioning` and `messaging-architecture` will serve better. Challenge premature category creation directly: if the product fits an existing category, say so.

## Core Principle: Category Kings vs. Category Competitors

**Category kings** define the problem, name the category, and become the default answer. They capture 70-80% of market cap, even as "better" competitors emerge. **Category competitors** fight for the remaining 20-30% — even with a better product, they're permanently playing catch-up in a market someone else defined.

Buyers take cognitive shortcuts. Once a category exists with a leader, they evaluate everyone else against that leader. If someone else defines the category, you're forever explaining how you compare to them. If you define it, you set the comparison criteria. Be the Salesforce, not the Siebel.

## The Five Elements

1. **The Problem** — define a problem that doesn't have a name yet
2. **The Point of View (POV)** — articulate the worldview that makes your approach inevitable
3. **The Category Name** — name the new market you're creating
4. **The Ecosystem** — map the players with a stake in the category succeeding
5. **The Lightning Strike** — design the moment that puts the category on the map

## Approach
Test first whether category design is right at all, then work through the five elements in order: problem frame, POV, name, ecosystem, lightning strike, and finally the flywheel plan. Interview the user throughout — first answers are usually too vague; probe for specificity, but recognize a real answer when you hear one. The output is a category design document covering all five elements plus a year-by-year flywheel plan.

### Element 1: The Problem

Category design starts with a problem, not a product. The problem you name must:
- Be real and acute for the customers you're targeting
- Exist independently of your product — the problem was there before your solution
- Currently have no satisfying solution (or the existing solutions are what's creating it)
- Be important enough that a market would form around solving it

Evaluation criteria:
- If the problem only exists with your product, it's not a category problem — it's a product problem
- Small problems make small categories; is the cost of the problem big enough to build a market around?
- Is there evidence customers experience this problem, even if they don't articulate it in your words?
- If the problem already has a name, what's the existing category and why is it inadequate?

Red flags: the problem is "people need better X" (that's competitive positioning, not a new problem); the problem is visible only from inside your company; it only matters to a tiny segment; existing category names already describe it acceptably.

The output of this phase contrasts the old frame (what the market currently calls this and why that framing is wrong) with the new problem frame, backed by evidence the problem is real.

### Element 2: The Point of View

The POV is not a product pitch. It's a declaration of how the world is changing, why existing approaches are inadequate, and what the right response looks like — the intellectual foundation of the category. A strong POV has four parts:

1. **The world has changed** — a shift in technology, behavior, economics, or regulation that customers are experiencing
2. **The old way no longer works** — why approaches built for the old world now create problems
3. **The new way** — the right approach, described in outcomes and principles, not your product
4. **The stakes** — what companies that adapt will achieve vs. those that don't

The test: if you published the POV and a smart prospect read it, would they immediately recognize it as true — even if they'd never articulated it? And the POV should hold up even if your company didn't exist.

**Relationship to Raskin's strategic narrative:** Andy Raskin's framework (`raskin-pitch`) covers similar territory, but his narrative is designed for sales pitches. The POV is a public declaration meant to recruit an entire ecosystem, educate a market, and define a category — bigger and longer-lived.

### Element 3: The Category Name

The name is strategic, not just descriptive. It should signal the new problem, differentiate from adjacent categories without a long explanation, be memorable enough that prospects repeat it, and be broad enough to expand into but narrow enough to own.

Naming approaches: outcome-based (name what customers achieve), problem-based (name the problem being solved), or approach-based (name the new way of working).

Tests for a candidate name:
- Can a target customer hear it and understand roughly what it addresses?
- Does it signal something meaningfully different from adjacent categories?
- Is it ownable — not already claimed by a large incumbent?
- Could a coherent Wikipedia article exist under this title in three years?
- Would analysts adopt it? Analyst adoption is a major signal of category traction.

Category names take time. The question isn't "do people understand it today?" but "will they understand it when the market matures?" — you're funding a multi-year education campaign.

### Element 4: The Ecosystem

Category kings don't succeed alone. A mature category has players who all benefit from its growth: customers, technology partners (better-together combinations), service partners who build practices around it, analysts who cover it, press who write about it, investors who fund it, practitioners who build professional identity around it, and thought leaders who educate the market.

Map each player and — critically — their specific stake: what's in it for them if the category grows. Worth probing: "Who are the ten partners who benefit most if this category grows?" and "Which analysts cover adjacent spaces and would logically extend into this one?"

### Element 5: The Lightning Strike

A lightning strike is a concentrated, coordinated event that makes the category real in the market's mind. Not a campaign — a moment. Everything else is slow-burn education; the strike is the ignition.

Forms it can take: a company-defined event (Salesforce's "No Software" protest at Siebel's conference), an industry keynote declaring the category, a research report that names and quantifies it, a high-profile customer announcement, a manifesto or book, a bold earned-media moment.

Design principles:
- **Air war + ground war together.** Coordinated external visibility (press, social, earned media) plus direct customer and ecosystem engagement (events, partner activation, direct outreach). One without the other doesn't work.
- **Timing matters.** Too early and it's ignored; too late and someone else captures the moment. The best time is when the problem is widely felt but the category hasn't yet crystallized.
- **Category, not product.** Salesforce wasn't demoing at Siebel's conference — they were making a statement about a category change.

Plan the strike with explicit success criteria: what does "it worked" look like at 30 days? Third parties adopting the category language and analyst coverage of the new category are the strongest signals.

## The Category Design Flywheel

Once the strike lands, momentum builds:

```
POV creates awareness
→ awareness attracts customers and partners
→ customers and partners create proof
→ proof attracts analysts and press
→ analysts and press validate the category
→ validation attracts more customers and competitors
→ competitors' entry proves the category is real
→ category king captures most of the value
```

The counterintuitive truth: when competitors enter your category, they validate it. Category kings welcome competition — it means the market is growing. The risk is waiting too long to establish dominance before they arrive.

Plan the flywheel in yearly horizons: what must be established in year one, what the category looks like when established in year two, and what confirms category-king status in year three.

## Common Pitfalls

**Category design as rebranding.** Renaming what you do without changing how you frame the problem. Start with the problem you're naming, not the name you want for your product.

**POV that's really a product pitch.** "We believe in [our product approach]" is differentiation, not a worldview. The POV should be true even if your company didn't exist.

**Category name nobody understands.** Test it with ten target customers; if more than half are deeply confused, simplify. Insider language in the POV fails the same way — it must be legible to a smart outsider.

**Lightning strike without ecosystem.** A flashy event with no partners, analysts, or customers to amplify it. Build the ecosystem before the strike, not after.

**Category design without commitment.** Starting, then retreating to competitive positioning when results are slow. Expect confusion before clarity and no immediate pipeline — or don't start.

**A narrative mistaken for a category.** A good story is necessary but not sufficient; you need ecosystem proof.
