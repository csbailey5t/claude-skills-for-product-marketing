# April Dunford Positioning Framework

## Overview
April Dunford's positioning methodology from "Obviously Awesome". Positioning is built from five interdependent components worked in a fixed order — each component is derived from the one before it, which is what keeps the result grounded in real customer decisions rather than internal wishful thinking.

## When to Use This Skill
- Positioning a new product or feature
- Repositioning an existing product
- Entering a new market segment
- Responding to competitive shifts
- Clarifying messaging that isn't resonating (fix positioning first, then use messaging-architecture)

## The Five Components, in Order

1. **Competitive Alternatives** — what customers would do if your solution didn't exist
2. **Unique Attributes** — capabilities you have that those alternatives don't
3. **Value (and Proof)** — the outcomes those attributes enable
4. **Target Market Characteristics** — who cares most about that value
5. **Market Category** — the context that makes your value obvious

The ordering is the method, not a convenience. Differentiation only exists relative to alternatives; value only exists as a consequence of real differentiation; the best-fit market is defined by who cares most about that value; and the category is chosen last, to frame everything that came before. Teams that start with "what makes us different?" or "what category are we in?" get positioning disconnected from any actual buying decision.

### 1. Competitive Alternatives
Key question: what would customers use or do if your product didn't exist?

Competitive alternatives are not the same as your sales competitors. They include spreadsheets, manual processes, building something in-house, hiring someone to do it, and — very often — doing nothing and living with the problem. A spreadsheet or the status quo is frequently the true competition, and the rest of the positioning falls apart if you skip past that to the vendors you see in deals.

Worth probing: what did customers use before adopting you, and when prospects say no, what do they actually do instead?

### 2. Unique Attributes
Key question: what can customers do with your product that they can't do with those alternatives?

Include both feature-level differences and architectural or approach-level ones. Attributes must be genuinely unique versus the alternatives from step 1, and specific enough to be defensible — "easy to use" is not an attribute. Some of the most differentiating capabilities are ones customers don't immediately recognize as valuable.

### 3. Value and Proof
Key question: what business outcomes do those attributes enable?

Value is outcome language, not feature language — "real-time sync" is a feature; "your team always acts on current data" is value. Multiple attributes often ladder up to a single value theme, and two or three themes is usually plenty; more dilutes the story. Back each theme with proof: metrics, customer quotes, case studies.

### 4. Target Market Characteristics
Key question: who cares a lot about that value?

Describe the characteristics that make a buyer care intensely — their situation, current pain, and buying triggers — not just firmographics. Good target market definitions feel narrow and specific; it's better to strongly own a small segment than to poorly serve a broad one.

Worth probing: what's happening in the best-fit customer's business that makes this urgent right now?

### 5. Market Category
Key question: what market context makes your value obvious to those customers?

Category choice sets buyer expectations, the comparison set, and pricing anchors. Pick the category where your unique strengths are assumed to matter, not the one that's trendy. Sometimes the right move is a subcategory or a new category — but only when existing ones actively hide your value. Test category names with prospects: does the name clarify what you do or confuse it?

## Positioning Statement Template

```
For [target market with specific characteristics]
Who [have this compelling reason to buy]
[Product name] is a [market category]
That [provides this unique value/benefit]
Unlike [competitive alternatives]
We [key differentiator]
```

## Common Pitfalls

**Starting with differentiation.** "What makes us different?" without first fixing the alternatives produces feature-focused positioning disconnected from customer value. Alternatives come first.

**Anchoring on sales competitors.** The vendors you see in late-stage deals may not be what most customers actually compare you to. Ask lost prospects what they did instead.

**Confusing features with value.** If the "value" statement could appear on a spec sheet, it's a feature. Keep translating until it's an outcome the buyer cares about.

**Too many value themes.** Trying to be everything to everyone dilutes positioning. Prioritize the two or three themes that resonate most with the target segment.

**Target market too broad.** "Enterprises" is not a target market. Narrow until the description predicts who will care.

**Aspirational positioning.** Positioning for the product you want to have rather than the one you have. Position for today; repositioning later is normal.

**Forcing a trendy category.** A category chosen for buzz rather than clarity confuses buyers. If prospects need the category explained away, it's the wrong category.

## Working With Someone on Positioning

Work the five components in order and resist jumping ahead — later answers change when earlier ones are done honestly. First answers are usually too vague — probe for specificity, but recognize a real answer when you hear one. Prefer evidence from real deals and customer language over assumptions, and flag gaps: value that doesn't trace back to an attribute, a target market that doesn't obviously care about the value, a category that hides the differentiation. At the end, read the whole positioning back as one story and check that it coheres. Positioning shouldn't be written alone — encourage involving sales, product, and actual customers.
